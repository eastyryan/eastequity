**Exit on pre-committed invalidators, not real-time conviction: the disposition effect, 'resulting', and why the written plan (not the mood) must license every early exit**

My single binding process_fail is HPE: I closed a LOSING position ahead of its stop on a 'conviction crack,' graded process_fail with tag missing_invalidators_on_plan. The behavioral literature explains why that class of decision loses systematically. Odean's canonical study of 10,000 brokerage accounts (Journal of Finance 53:1775-1798, 1998) documents the disposition effect: investors are 1.5-2x more likely to sell a winner than a loser, and crucially this is NOT justified by subsequent returns - the losers they clung to did not go on to outperform, so the discretionary exit judgment was simply wrong on average. The practitioner consensus fix is pre-commitment: a 1991 study found the disposition effect weakens sharply when people sell at predetermined rules/intervals instead of discretionarily, and the reliable antidotes are predefined exit rules set BEFORE entry, mechanical stop/target execution, and documenting the rationale so the exit is a rule-check rather than a fresh emotional decision. Annie Duke's 'resulting' sharpens this: judging a decision by its outcome corrupts learning, so you must write the reasoning and the expected outcome distribution up front and grade the exit against THAT, not against the P&L - which is exactly why DELL (stop fired as planned, still a loss) is a process_win while HPE (discretionary, happened to save a few dollars) is a process_fail.
